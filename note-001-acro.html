NOTE_001 · CYBERSECURITY GOVERNANCE

The ACRO cyber attack: why responsibility matters.

What stood out to me about the ACRO cyber incident was not only the technical vulnerability, but the lack of clarity around who was responsible for dealing with it.

CYBERSECURITY GOVERNANCE PATCH MANAGEMENT RISK

What happened?

I recently came across the Information Commissioner's Office investigation into the cyber attack affecting the ACRO Criminal Records Office.

The attacker had gained unauthorised access to ACRO's public-facing website and content management system. The incident potentially affected thousands of people and involved information that could include sensitive personal data.

When I first read about the incident, I expected the main issue to be something highly technical. Instead, one of the things that stood out to me most was how much basic security management and responsibility mattered.

The part that surprised me

One of the issues identified was patch management.

A security patch is essentially an update that fixes a known vulnerability or weakness in software. If an important patch is not applied, that weakness can remain available for an attacker to exploit.

What surprised me more was that there was not enough clarity around who was responsible for identifying and monitoring important security updates.

ACRO used third-party providers for some security services, including patch management, but the ICO found that responsibility for critical updates was not clearly managed.

Before reading about this, I assumed an organisation handling highly sensitive information would always have a clearly defined person, team or provider responsible for something as important as security updates.

Why I found it interesting

This made me think differently about cybersecurity.

When people first think about cyber attacks, it is easy to focus on hackers, malware, vulnerabilities and technical security tools. Those things are important, but this incident showed me how much security also depends on processes, communication and accountability.

An organisation can have security technology in place, but if nobody clearly owns a task, alerts are not properly investigated, or vulnerabilities are not dealt with, weaknesses can still remain.

It also made the governance and risk side of cybersecurity more interesting to me because it connects technical security with how an organisation actually operates.

Another thing I noticed

The incident also showed that security controls can still make a difference even when an attack succeeds.

ACRO's network segmentation helped prevent the attacker from moving from the compromised website environment into its core systems.

I found this useful because it shows that cybersecurity is not always about completely preventing every possible attack. It is also about limiting what an attacker can reach and reducing the impact if something does go wrong.

What I took from it

My biggest takeaway was that technical security and governance cannot really be separated.

Patch management, monitoring, clear responsibilities and technical controls all have to work together. A weakness in one part of that process can affect everything else.

As I continue learning cybersecurity, this is something I want to understand more: not only how attacks happen technically, but how organisations build processes that reduce the chance of those attacks succeeding in the first place.
